Our view

Investment thesis

Marvell competes with Broadcom in custom accelerator silicon and trades at 46.3x forward earnings against Broadcom's 20.8x — more than double the multiple for the weaker competitive position. Revenue is growing 27.6% but earnings per share fell 80.4% in the latest quarter, and the 14.5% operating margin sits well below Broadcom's 49%. The custom-silicon theme is real and we are Overweight the leader in it; we are Underweight the follower at twice the price. The qualifier we take seriously: Marvell has genuine design wins and a credible optical interconnect franchise, so this is a relative valuation view rather than a bet against the business.

The edge, if there is one

What the market is missing

In a two-horse market, the multiple ranking should follow the execution ranking, and here it is inverted. Broadcom earns 49% operating margins on custom silicon; Marvell earns 14.5%. That gap is the market telling you who has the pricing power and who is competing for scraps of it. Yet Marvell carries the higher multiple, which only makes sense if you believe it is about to close the gap. The EPS decline of 80% points the other way.

Second, the earnings decline is being explained away as investment. Perhaps. But when a company in a supposedly booming end market posts an 80% EPS decline, the burden of proof sits with the bullish interpretation, and it has not been met with disclosure that would settle it.

Held to account

What would prove us wrong

Specific and monitorable. If any of these prints, we change the view in public.

  3. EPS returns to year-on-year growth — the direct refutation of the earnings-quality concern.

Teaching note

The lesson is operating margin as the scoreboard in a competitive duel. When two companies compete for the same customers with similar products, the margin gap tells you who has pricing power far more reliably than revenue growth does. Anyone can grow revenue by pricing aggressively; only the company with a genuine advantage grows revenue and holds margin. So in any competitive pair, put the two margin lines side by side first, before you look at growth, market share, or product roadmaps. The margin gap usually settles the argument that the product comparison cannot.

Second: be sceptical when revenue growth and earnings move sharply in opposite directions. It may be genuine investment, but it may be that the growth is being purchased. The disclosure required to tell those apart is often absent, and absence of that disclosure is itself information.
