Our view

Investment thesis

Arm's architecture licensing model is genuinely outstanding — high gross margin, low capital intensity, and a royalty stream that compounds with every device shipped. But the stock trades at 108.7x forward earnings with a 7.6% operating margin and a beta of 3.77, the highest in our coverage. Revenue is growing 22.4%, which is respectable and nowhere near enough to justify that multiple. The royalty transition to newer architecture versions is real and will raise per-device economics over time, but it is a gradual mechanical process, not a step change, and the current price appears to discount several years of it happening at once. This is a valuation call on an excellent business, and the 3.77 beta means it will be violently right or wrong.

The edge, if there is one

What the market is missing

The operating margin is the disconnect. A pure licensing business with a 96%+ gross margin should convert far more of its revenue to operating profit than 7.6%. The gap is operating expense — Arm is spending heavily on its own compute subsystems and pushing further up the value chain. That may be strategically correct, but it converts a capital-light royalty model into something considerably more ordinary, and the multiple still reflects the former.

Second, the free float is unusually small, which amplifies moves in both directions and helps explain both the ascent and a beta of 3.77. Where a valuation is supported partly by float scarcity rather than fundamentals, it is inherently unstable — scarcity premia unwind quickly when sentiment turns, and 95.4% institutional ownership means the marginal seller is a large one.

Teaching note

The transferable point is about business model quality versus business model execution. Arm's model is genuinely superb on paper: license once, collect royalties forever, almost no capital required. But the reported operating margin is 7.6%, because the company is spending the gross profit on expanding its scope. The lesson is to always check whether a celebrated business model is actually showing up in the margins. A great model that is being reinvested into an ordinary one is, for now, an ordinary business — and should be valued as one until the reinvestment proves out.

Second, note the market-structure observation: small free float amplifies everything. When 95% of a company is held by institutions and the float is thin, price discovery is poor in both directions. That is a genuine risk factor and it belongs in position sizing, not just in a footnote.
