Our view

Investment thesis

Alphabet trades at roughly 17x forward earnings with revenue growing 24% and a 55% net profit margin, which makes it the cheapest of the megacaps on forward earnings while being the only one that is vertically integrated across silicon (TPU), models, cloud and consumer distribution. The market's objection is capex — $175–185bn guided for 2026, and a Q2 report that triggered a sector-wide selloff — and that objection is legitimate: free cash flow is down 38% year-on-year and the depreciation wave has barely started to land. But Alphabet is funding this from operating cash flow rather than debt, and its TPU programme means a meaningful fraction of that capex is captured internally rather than paid to a supplier at a 75% gross margin. We think the market is charging Alphabet the full cost of the AI build-out while giving it no credit for being the only buyer that is also, partially, its own supplier. At 17x, we are not required to be right about much for this to work.

The edge, if there is one

What the market is missing

The TPU is a cost structure, not a science project. Every other hyperscaler buys its accelerators at a supplier's gross margin. Alphabet builds a large share of its own. In a world where the market has just decided that the cost of AI infrastructure is the central problem, the company with structurally lower unit costs should be re-rating relative to peers — and instead it de-rated alongside them. That is the mispricing.

Second, the search-disruption discount has not been removed even though the evidence has moved. Alphabet trades at a discount to Microsoft on forward earnings despite faster revenue growth and a higher net margin. The residual reason is the standing fear that generative search destroys the query-monetisation model. Two years in, revenue growth has accelerated, not decelerated. At some point a discount for a risk that has not materialised becomes a source of return rather than a reason for caution — though we note honestly that "it hasn't happened yet" is a weak argument against a structural risk, and we hold that tension openly.

Third, the 294% year-on-year jump in quarterly EPS is not being priced as durable — and it probably should not be, in full. But even normalising heavily, the forward multiple is the lowest in the megacap group by a wide margin.

Held to account

What would prove us wrong

Specific and monitorable. If any of these prints, we change the view in public.

Specific and monitorable. Agent A3 (Thesis Guardian) watches these. Vague conditions are useless.

  1. Search revenue growth falls below 8% for two consecutive quarters. That would be the first hard evidence that generative interfaces are cannibalising monetisation rather than expanding it.
  2. Cloud operating margin declines for two consecutive quarters while capex keeps rising — the signal that the build-out is not earning its cost of capital.
  3. TPU deployment share stops rising in disclosed capacity commentary, indicating the internal-silicon cost advantage is not scaling.

Valuation conclusion

17.4x forward for 24% revenue growth and a 55% net margin is the widest growth-to-multiple gap in our megacap coverage. We apply 21x to a forward EPS of about $20.50 for a $425 target, ~19% above the indicative price. We stay below the consensus $427 only marginally here — this is one of the few names where we and the street broadly agree, and we note that agreement reduces the value of the call. The differentiated part of our view is not the target but the reason: we are underwriting the TPU cost advantage, which consensus largely treats as optionality.

Sector-specific read:

Search revenue growth (the disruption test), Cloud revenue growth and Cloud operating margin together (growth alone is uninformative when capex is this large), capex as a percentage of operating cash flow, and any disclosure on TPU deployment share. Watch depreciation as a percentage of revenue quarter by quarter — that is the wall arriving.

Expectations & earnings reaction pattern

The Q2 report "sparked a sell-off" across the sector, which tells us the bar had been set on capex restraint rather than on earnings. Alphabet beat on earnings and fell on spending. That is the clearest possible statement of what the market is currently rewarding: discipline, not growth. Until that flips, Alphabet needs to demonstrate that capex is decelerating or that Cloud margins are expanding — beating on EPS will not be enough.

Above both moving averages and holding better than the semiconductor complex, which is consistent with our reading that this is a financing-quality selloff rather than an AI-demand selloff: the balance sheets that can absorb the spend are being punished less. Relative strength versus the SOX is the cleanest confirmation we have that the market is discriminating.

Reminder: a stock with perfect fundamentals that the market has decided to dislike will not go up. Momentum is evidence, not noise.

Management & guidance behaviour

Guidance tracker not yet built — flagged to B2. Observable behaviour: Alphabet discloses less operational detail than peers (no TPU unit economics, limited Cloud segment granularity), which is a genuine analytical handicap and should be treated as a mild negative on transparency grounds. Capital allocation has shifted decisively from buybacks toward capex; the dividend remains token. Management has been willing to accept visible free-cash-flow deterioration to fund the build, which is either conviction or a lack of discipline and we cannot yet tell which.

Teaching note

This name teaches vertical integration as a valuation input. Analysts habitually treat in-house component development as a footnote — a capability, not a number. But when an entire sector's problem becomes the cost of a component, the company that makes its own suddenly has a different cost curve than its peers, and that belongs in the model rather than the appendix. The general habit to build: whenever the market's dominant worry shifts (here, from "who has the best AI product?" to "who can afford AI?"), immediately re-rank the sector on the new question. Most of the mispricing after a narrative shift comes from rankings that were built for the old one and never rebuilt.
