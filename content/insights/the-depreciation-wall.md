There is a number that explains more about the last six weeks in technology markets than any
amount of commentary about AI sentiment, and it is an accounting number.

In the four quarters to March 2026, the four largest US hyperscalers purchased approximately
**$433.9bn of property and equipment**. Over the same period they recognised roughly **$149bn
of depreciation**.

The difference — about $285bn — has not gone away. It is sitting on balance sheets, waiting to
be expensed on a schedule that was fixed the moment the assets were bought.

This piece is about what that means, why it is arriving now, and why it explains the otherwise
baffling fact that semiconductor equities have fallen into a bear market while the price of the
scarcest component in the entire supply chain keeps going up.

---

## What depreciation actually does

Start with the mechanics, because the intuition matters more than the arithmetic.

When a company buys an asset it expects to use for years, it does not charge the whole cost
against this year's profit. That would make a growing company look catastrophically unprofitable
and a shrinking one look excellent. Instead the cost is spread across the asset's useful life.

So a $6bn datacentre building, on a 30-year life, costs the income statement $200m a year.

The critical consequence: **when spending is growing fast, reported profit looks much better than
cash reality**, because the income statement is still recognising the small purchases of five
years ago while the cash flow statement records the enormous purchases of today.

That gap is not a distortion. It reverses. Every dollar of the gap eventually becomes an expense.
The only question is when.

## Why AI capex is different from previous cycles

Here is where the standard mental model breaks, and where most of the analytical error is being
made right now.

Investors have a well-worn heuristic for large technology capex programmes: Amazon spent
aggressively on fulfilment centres and won; Microsoft and Google spent aggressively on datacentres
and won. The heuristic says *spend through the scepticism, the assets compound.*

Those assets had 20-to-40-year lives.

**Roughly two-thirds of Microsoft's recent capex went into short-lived assets — GPUs and CPUs, on
three-to-five year schedules.**

That single fact changes the economics completely. Consider two companies, each spending $100bn:

| | Long-lived assets (30yr) | Short-lived assets (4yr) |
|---|---|---|
| Annual depreciation | $3.3bn | **$25bn** |
| Time until full expense recognition | 30 years | **4 years** |
| Reinvestment needed to hold capacity | Minimal for decades | **$100bn again in year 5** |

The second column is not an investment in the traditional sense. It is closer to a **recurring
operating cost that happens to be paid in advance**. And a recurring cost of that magnitude has to
be covered by recurring revenue, not by a story about the future.

This is the difference between building a railway and leasing a fleet of vans. Both are capex.
Only one of them compounds.

## The evidence that it has started

The theory would be interesting but academic if the numbers had not already turned. They have:

| | Trailing twelve-month free cash flow |
|---|---|
| **Amazon** | ~$26bn a year ago → **~$1.2bn** |
| **Alphabet** | **−38%** year-on-year |
| **Microsoft** | **−22%** year-on-year |

Amazon's free cash flow has fallen by roughly 95%. This is a company whose entire valuation
framework for two decades rested on the argument that reported earnings understated its true cash
generation.

And there is a second, sharper signal hiding in plain sight on any quote screen.

**Amazon's forward P/E (about 32x) is higher than its trailing P/E (about 22x).**

Take a moment with that. A forward multiple above a trailing multiple means consensus expects
earnings per share to **fall**. Analysts are not modelling deceleration in growth — they are
modelling an outright decline in profit, at a company growing revenue at nearly 20%.

That is the depreciation wall, translated into an estimate.

## Why this made semiconductors fall while memory prices rose

Now the apparent paradox resolves.

Over the same weeks that the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell into a bear market and chip
equities lost more than $1trn in value:

- Third-quarter DRAM contract prices settled **higher**, with cumulative year-to-date average
  selling price increases in the region of **340%**
- 2026 HBM supply is reported **sold out**
- TSMC's revenue grew **36%** year-on-year
- The four hyperscalers guided to roughly **$725bn** of 2026 capex, up about **77%**

None of that is a demand problem. Demand indicators are, if anything, accelerating.

What the market repriced was not whether the chips would be bought. It was **whether the buyers'
balance sheets could keep buying them** — and, crucially, what happens to those balance sheets
when the depreciation from the current build-out lands at the same time as the replacement cycle
for the first wave of GPUs.

The selloff was a **credit-quality judgement expressed through equity prices.** That is why it hit
indiscriminately: when a market re-rates an entire category of risk, it sells everything with the
label on it before it works out which names actually carry the risk.

## Where the risk actually sits

This is the part that matters for a portfolio, and it is where we think the market has been
sloppy.

A financing problem is not symmetric. It is bad for whoever must **fund** the spending, and merely
slower for whoever **receives** it. So the useful question for every company caught in this
selloff is simple:

> **Does this company pay, or does it get paid?**

Sort the AI complex on that question and it separates cleanly:

**Getting paid, in cash, now, into a constrained order book:** the accelerator designers, the
foundry, the memory producers, the equipment vendors, the power and cooling suppliers. They
receive the capex. They carry no build-out debt. Their 2026 revenue is largely already committed.

**Paying, with balance sheets under visible strain:** the hyperscalers absorbing the depreciation,
and — at the extreme — Oracle, which carries roughly $124.7bn of debt, raised $43bn in debt and
$5bn in equity in the fiscal year to May 2026, plans about $40bn more in FY27, and disclosed in
its own annual report that some customers "may be highly leveraged" with attendant non-payment
risk.

The market has been marking both groups down together. That is where we think the opportunity is
— and to be clear about the risk we are taking: **if hyperscaler capex is genuinely cut in 2027,
the companies getting paid today are the first casualties tomorrow.** We think that is beyond a
12-month horizon because 2026 HBM is sold out and the capex is contractually committed. That is a
judgement about *timing*, not a disagreement with the bear case.

## What would tell you the wall has broken through

Three things, in order of how early they would appear:

1. **Sequential DRAM contract prices turning negative.** The highest-frequency signal available.
   Rising prices with falling equities is a divergence; falling prices with falling equities is a
   confirmation, and the supply chain thesis ends.

2. **Any hyperscaler guiding 2027 capex below 2026.** One is noise. Two is a regime change, and
   the entire supply chain re-rates.

3. **Depreciation as a percentage of revenue accelerating faster than AI revenue.** The direct
   measurement. It is disclosed quarterly and almost nobody tracks it as a series.

---

## The transferable lesson

Strip away the specifics and there are three habits here worth keeping.

**Check whether a company's capex is building assets or renting time.** The word "investment"
covers both a railway and a van lease. Useful life is the variable that separates them, and it is
disclosed. A company converting operating cost into capitalised, short-lived assets is not
compounding; it is smoothing.

**When forward P/E exceeds trailing P/E, stop and find out why.** It takes five seconds and it
tells you the market expects earnings to fall. It is one of the most information-dense comparisons
available and almost nobody makes it, because screens display one multiple at a time.

**When a sector sells off on a single narrative, ask who pays and who gets paid.** Markets
initially mark down everything with the label attached. The distinction between the funder and the
recipient of a spending programme is where the mispricing lives, and it usually persists for weeks
rather than days — long enough to act on.
