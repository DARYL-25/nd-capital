Most valuation commentary stops at the adjective. A stock is "expensive" at 56x sales, or "cheap"
at 5x earnings, and the conversation moves on.

That is not analysis. It is a description with a tone of voice attached.

A multiple is the beginning of a question, and the question is always the same: **what does this
company have to actually do, operationally, for this price to make sense?** Answering it converts
an opinion into something falsifiable — a claim about revenue, or margin, or years, that reality
will eventually settle.

This piece walks through the conversion on four names in our coverage. The technique matters more
than the conclusions.

---

## Step one: the reverse test

Take Palantir. At the time of writing it trades at roughly **56x trailing sales** — *after*
falling about 41% from its high. Saying "that's expensive" adds nothing; everyone knows.

So invert it. Hold margins constant and ask what revenue would be required to reach a multiple
that is merely *high* rather than extraordinary — say 10x sales:

> Revenue must grow **5.6-fold.**

Now put a clock on it. At 84.7% annual growth — an exceptional rate that very few companies have
ever sustained — 5.6-fold takes **2.8 years**.

So the sentence you can actually test is:

> *To reach a 10x sales multiple, Palantir must compound at 85% for the best part of three years
> without stumbling — and that only gets it to expensive.*

That is a claim you can be wrong about. It invites the right follow-up questions: has the company
ever done that? Is the addressable market large enough to permit it? What happens if growth
decelerates to merely excellent — say 50%?

Compare that to "56x sales is a lot." One of these is research.

---

## Step two: work the denominator, not the ratio

Now the opposite case, and the more dangerous one.

Micron trades at about **5.3x forward earnings**. Every screen in the world flags that as cheap.

It is not cheap. It is a **warning**.

A 5.3x forward multiple on a company whose revenue grew 346% year-on-year is the market making an
explicit, considered statement: *these earnings are the peak and they will not persist.* The
market is not mispricing the E. It is telling you the E is temporary.

This is the single most common error in cyclical investing. The correct response to a very low
multiple is never "look how cheap." It is:

> **What does the market think normalised earnings are, and do I have a better estimate?**

That reframes the entire exercise. You are no longer arguing about a ratio; you are arguing about
a number — mid-cycle earnings power — which is a question you can research. Contract prices,
capacity additions, supply discipline, the sold-out horizon. All of that bears on the denominator.
None of it bears on the ratio.

**Work the denominator. The ratio will take care of itself.**

---

## Step three: check the first derivative

Qualcomm trades at about **14x forward earnings** with a 2.4% dividend yield and a PEG of 0.63.
By every static measure it is the cheapest name in our coverage.

It also has **declining revenue** (−4% year-on-year) and **declining EPS** (−23%), because its
largest customer is progressively replacing its chips with in-house silicon.

A low multiple on *growing* earnings is cheap. A low multiple on *shrinking* earnings is the
market correctly identifying a business in decline. They look identical on a screen and they are
completely different investments.

So before reacting to any multiple, look at the direction of the denominator. Is E rising or
falling? A 20x multiple on rising earnings and a 20x multiple on falling earnings share a number
and nothing else.

The practical rule we use: **before buying anything on a low multiple, require two consecutive
quarters of stabilising or growing revenue.** You will miss the exact bottom. You will also avoid
the much larger category of things that looked cheap the whole way down.

## Step four: compare within the tightest possible set

Broad comparisons are nearly worthless. "Semiconductors trade at X" tells you almost nothing,
because the category contains memory producers, equipment vendors, fabless designers and foundries
— businesses with entirely different economics.

The value is in tight sets. Consider two names in the same industry, selling to the same customers,
in the same cycle:

| | NVIDIA | AMD |
|---|---|---|
| Forward P/E | **~23x** | **~66x** |
| Operating margin | **65.6%** | 14.4% |
| Return on equity | **114%** | 8.1% |
| Revenue growth | **+85%** | +38% |

You do not need a view on whether semiconductors are cheap to find this interesting. Nearly all
the shared risk — the cycle, export controls, hyperscaler capex, macro — is common to both and
cancels out. What remains is the comparison itself.

**Build your comp sets to be as narrow as possible.** A comps table with twenty names has usually
explained nothing; a table with three has isolated a variable.

## Step five: work out what the risk is priced at

The last technique is the most useful and the least practised.

Salesforce trades at about **13.5x forward earnings** — a multiple normally associated with
declining industrials, not a software franchise with 78% gross margins whose EPS grew 52% last
quarter. The de-rating reflects a real fear: that agentic AI collapses seat-based licensing.

Everyone knows about that risk. It is discussed constantly. Which is precisely why it is unlikely
to be a source of return on the short side — **consensus risks are, almost by definition, in the
price.**

So the question is never "is this risk real?" It is:

> **What multiple would this stock trade at if the risk were certain — and how does that compare
> to the actual multiple?**

If a disrupted, ex-growth Salesforce is worth 11x and it trades at 13.5x while still growing, the
market has priced the disruption as roughly an accomplished fact. You are then being paid to take
the other side, and you do not even need consensus to be *wrong* — only early.

That is a completely different investment proposition from "the market is too pessimistic," and it
is arrived at by pricing the scenario rather than arguing about the mood.

---

## The five questions

Every multiple in our research goes through the same sequence:

1. **Reverse it.** What revenue, margin or time period does this price require? State it as a
   sentence someone could disprove.
2. **Work the denominator.** Is the E sustainable? What is mid-cycle? A low multiple usually means
   the market disagrees with your E, not that it has stopped paying attention.
3. **Check the first derivative.** Is the denominator rising or falling? This separates cheap from
   a value trap.
4. **Compare narrowly.** Same industry, same customers, same cycle. Let the shared risks cancel.
5. **Price the risk.** What would the multiple be if the bear case were certain? Compare to today.

None of this requires better data than anyone else has. All of it requires refusing to stop at the
adjective.
