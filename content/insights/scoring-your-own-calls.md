We have published 24 ratings and resolved none of them. Coverage was initiated on 2 August 2026,
so there is no track record on this site yet and there will not be one for some months.

That makes this a good moment to write down how we intend to score ourselves — **before** we know
the answers, while it is still costless to be strict. Scoring rules written after the results are
in are not scoring rules. They are excuses with a methodology section.

---

## Why hit rate is a weak measure

Hit rate is the percentage of calls that came good. It is the number every track record leads with,
and on its own it is close to meaningless.

The problem is that it is trivially gameable in the direction of caution. An analyst who only ever
predicts that large, stable, widely-held businesses will muddle through will post an excellent hit
rate and never say anything anyone needed to hear. A high hit rate can be evidence of good
judgement, or it can be evidence of a refusal to take positions where being wrong is possible.

You cannot tell which from the number.

Worse, hit rate is silent on magnitude. Being right eleven times for one percent each and wrong
once for thirty percent is a losing record with a 92% hit rate.

So we track it — but never alone, and never first.

---

## The two scores we keep separate

Every call gets scored twice, on two axes that are deliberately not combined into one number.

### Outcome — did the price go where we said, over the horizon we said?

Recorded as **Correct / Partial / Wrong**, against a claim written in advance with a specific
evaluation date. The horizon is part of the claim. "Eventually" is not a prediction, and a call
that comes good two years after the stated window is not a hit; it is a different call that we did
not make.

### Reasoning — was the argument sound, independent of what happened?

Recorded as **Sound / Flawed / Lucky**. This is the axis that actually improves the process, and it
produces two entries that most track records refuse to make:

**Right for the wrong reason is logged as a failure.** If we said a stock would rise on margin
expansion and it rose on a takeover bid, we did not get that right. We got paid, which is a
different thing. Recording it as a win teaches the system to repeat a piece of reasoning that did
not work, and the next time there will be no takeover.

**Wrong for a sound reason is logged as such.** An argument that was correct on the evidence
available and was overturned by something genuinely unforeseeable is not the same mistake as an
argument that ignored a risk sitting in plain view. Collapsing those two into "wrong" destroys the
information you most need.

The grid is worth sitting with:

| | Outcome right | Outcome wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **Reasoning sound** | The process worked. Repeat it. | Acceptable. Variance exists. Check the horizon. |
| **Reasoning flawed** | **The dangerous quadrant.** Got paid, learned nothing, will repeat the error. | Honest failure. The most instructive cell in the table. |

Most research operations count the top-left and bottom-left as one column called "hit rate," and
in doing so they lose the only distinction that compounds.

## Calibration: the measure that is hard to fake

Calibration asks a different question: **when we say 30%, does it happen about 30% of the time?**

Every scenario table we publish carries explicit probabilities for bear, base and bull. Over enough
calls, those probabilities become testable. Group every scenario we assigned roughly 30% and count
how many occurred. If it is close to three in ten, our confidence signal means something. If it is
one in ten, we are systematically overconfident. If it is six in ten, we are hedging.

Calibration is hard to game because it punishes both directions. You cannot improve it by being
timid — assigning 50% to everything scores badly the moment outcomes are lopsided. And it can only
be measured if probabilities were stated in advance, in public, which is precisely why we publish
them before knowing anything.

A related check: **high-conviction calls must beat the overall hit rate.** If they do not, the
conviction label carries no information and should be removed rather than displayed. That is a test
our own system can fail, and we have written it into the scorecard so that it is checked
automatically rather than remembered selectively.

---

## What gets recorded, and when

The rule that makes the rest work: **the claim, the horizon and the disconfirming evidence are
written down before the outcome is known, and are never edited afterwards.**

Each entry carries the date it was made, the agent that made it, a falsifiable claim, a conviction
level, an evaluation date, and — once resolved — the outcome, the reasoning quality, the root cause
if wrong, and the lesson.

Two habits protect it:

**No quiet revisions.** When a view changes, the prior view stays on the record with the date and
the reason it changed. A change log where the old view is deleted is a marketing document. The
record of having been wrong is the most valuable data the system produces, and it is the first
thing an operation grading its own homework is tempted to lose.

**Disconfirming evidence, specified in advance.** Every rating we publish carries a list of what
would prove it wrong, specific enough to be monitored. This is not humility for its own sake. It is
what makes the eventual scoring possible: if you did not say in advance what would falsify the
claim, you can always find a reading of events under which you were basically right.

---

## The uncomfortable part

A scoring system built this way will produce a public record that looks worse than the ones you are
used to seeing, for three reasons: losses appear, right-for-the-wrong-reason is counted against us,
and there is no backtest to pad the early numbers.

That is the intended result. A track record that only appears once it looks good is not a track
record; it is a selection effect. Any research operation showing performance on day one is showing
you something that was fitted after the fact.

Ours currently reads: **24 open calls, none resolved, no hit rate, no calibration data.** It will
stay that way until there are outcomes to measure, and when there are, the ones that went against
us will be on the same page as the ones that did not.
