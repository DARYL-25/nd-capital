AI Compute & GPU

Merchant accelerators: the chips that train and serve the models, and the cycle everything else in technology is currently priced against.

Stage · Consensus — actively de-rating

This is the theme that has driven technology markets for three years: the merchant accelerators — overwhelmingly NVIDIA's — that hyperscalers, neoclouds and sovereign programmes buy to train and serve AI models. Demand has not visibly broken; the four largest US hyperscalers have guided to roughly $725bn of 2026 capex, up about 77% on 2025. What has broken is the market's willingness to fund that spending without asking what it returns, and that shift — from a demand question to a financing question — is what has put the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index into a bear market while order books remain full.

Be honest about lateness

Where we are in the lifecycle

Be honest about lateness. Most money in thematic investing is lost by arriving at "Crowded."

Consensus, and de-rating from it. Not "crowded" in the classic sense — crowded implies prices still rising on inflows, and prices are falling. This is the phase after crowded: positioning is being unwound while fundamentals are still good, which is characteristically where the largest single-name dispersion appears.

Evidence for this staging: chip equities shed more than $1trn in the late-July selloff; the Nasdaq-100 sits roughly 9.5% below its 2 June record; sell-side initiation volume peaked some time ago; and — the clearest signal — valuation dispersion within the theme is now extreme, with NVIDIA at about 23x forward against AMD at 66x and ARM at 109x. Indiscriminate buying produces narrow dispersion. Wide dispersion means the market has started discriminating, which is a late-consensus rather than an early-theme characteristic.

Second- and third-order beneficiaries

This is where the non-obvious money is. Who supplies the supplier?

This is where the non-obvious money is, and in this cycle it has moved twice.

First it moved to memory. HBM turned out to be the binding constraint on how fast accelerators could be fed, which dragged Korean names into an AI theme they had not previously belonged to. That is now well understood — though, remarkably, no longer reflected in the prices.

It is now moving to power and thermal. A rack that cannot be cooled cannot be deployed regardless of chip availability, and liquid-cooling retrofits are slow, capital-intensive and skills-constrained. Vertiv is the listed expression; the deeper second-order beneficiaries are electrical equipment, switchgear and grid interconnection — most of which sits in industrials sector codes and is therefore invisible to anyone screening technology.

The third move is forming and is not yet named: the financing layer. Who lends against assets that depreciate in three to five years, and at what spread. See the house view.

What the market is rewarding right now

Capital discipline, not growth. This changed during the current earnings season and it is the single most important observation in the theme. Alphabet reported, beat on earnings, raised capex — and triggered a sector-wide selloff. For three years the market rewarded whoever spent most aggressively. It now rewards whoever can show a return, or better still, whoever does not need to spend at all. Any model still assuming that capex announcements are bullish is calibrated to the previous regime.

The bear case for the theme itself

Argued at full strength: the $725bn of 2026 capex is being funded by companies whose free cash flow is collapsing under it — Amazon from roughly $26bn to $1.2bn, Alphabet down 38%, Microsoft down 22% — and the depreciation from it has barely begun to land. The four US hyperscalers bought about $433.9bn of property and equipment in the four quarters to March 2026 while recognising only about $149bn of depreciation. That gap arrives on future income statements whether or not AI revenue does. When it does, boards will face a choice between protecting margins and continuing to spend, and boards facing that choice have historically chosen margins. Roughly two-thirds of Microsoft's recent capex went into assets with three-to five-year lives — this is not a 25-year infrastructure investment being financed patiently, it is a rapidly-depreciating asset base requiring continuous reinvestment simply to stand still. If the revenue does not arrive before the second replacement cycle, the entire theme re-rates to industrial multiples.

We do not dismiss this. We think it is the correct long-run concern and simply not yet the 12-month one, because 2026 HBM is sold out and the capex is contractually committed. That is a timing judgement, not a disagreement, and it is recorded as an active contradiction in the house view.
