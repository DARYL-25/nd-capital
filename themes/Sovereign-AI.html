Sovereign AI

Governments building national AI capacity. Real money, long timelines, and announcements that considerably outpace deliveries.

Stage · Emerging

Sovereign AI describes national and regional programmes to build domestic AI compute capacity, motivated by data residency, security and industrial policy rather than by return on capital. That makes it a genuinely different demand vector from commercial capex — it is less price-sensitive, slower, and far less likely to be cut in a downturn. It is also the theme where announced figures diverge most from delivered capacity, and we treat announcements here with considerable scepticism.

Second- and third-order beneficiaries

This is where the non-obvious money is. Who supplies the supplier?

The genuinely useful characteristic of this theme is its low correlation with the commercial capex cycle. Sovereign programmes are funded by government budgets and justified by security rather than by return on invested capital, which means they are considerably less likely to be cut if hyperscaler capex moderates. In a market whose central worry is exactly that commercial-capex moderation, a demand source with different funding dynamics is a genuine diversifier — and it is not being valued as one.

What the market is rewarding right now

Deliveries, not announcements — appropriately. The market has largely stopped reacting to sovereign AI headlines, which is the correct calibration given the announce-to-install gap.

The bear case for the theme itself

Argued at full strength: government technology programmes are notorious for announcement inflation, multi-year delays and quiet cancellation. Much of the announced sovereign AI capacity may never be built, and what is built may be poorly utilised because the operational expertise to run large training clusters is scarce and concentrated in a handful of commercial organisations. Sovereign programmes are also politically reversible in a way commercial contracts are not: an election can cancel them. Treating announced sovereign capacity as a demand backstop is, on the historical record of government IT programmes, optimistic.
