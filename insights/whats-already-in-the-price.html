What's Already In the Price

Why a company can beat consensus and fall 10% on the same afternoon

AMDPLTRANETNVDAMUAMZN

Every earnings season produces the same confused headline: company beats estimates, stock falls.

It gets reported as irrationality. It is nothing of the sort. It is the most predictable thing in markets, and it happens because the number the company beat was not the number that mattered.

This piece is about finding the number that does.

The bar is not the published estimate

The consensus figure you see quoted is an average of published sell-side estimates. It is a real number, it is easy to find, and it is not what the stock is trading against.

Three things sit between it and the actual bar:

Staleness. Analysts update on their own schedule. By the time a company reports, some estimates in that average are weeks old and were built before the quarter's most important information existed. The average lags reality in exactly the direction reality moved.

The buy-side whisper. The people actually setting the price have their own numbers, and they are systematically higher than the published consensus for companies in a strong cycle. Nobody publishes the whisper. You infer it from positioning, from the implied move, and from how the stock traded into the print.

The guidance, not the quarter. For most technology companies the reported quarter is close to already known — channel checks, partner commentary and the previous guide have narrowed it. The information is in the next guide. A company can beat the quarter comfortably and guide two percent light and lose ten percent, because only one of those two numbers was still uncertain.

The published consensus tells you what analysts wrote down. The price tells you what investors believe. When those disagree, the price wins, because the price is where the money is.

How to read the bar before the print

You cannot observe the whisper directly. You can triangulate it from four things that are all public.

1. The implied move versus the realised move

Options markets price an expected move for the event. Compare it with what the stock has actually done, on average, over the last eight or twelve prints.

  • Implied well above realised — the event is richly priced. The market is nervous and a lot of the disappointment is already paid for.
  • Implied well below realised — the market is complacent about a stock that has a history of moving hard.

This does not tell you direction. It tells you how much surprise is already in the price, which is the more useful of the two.

2. The reaction history

Log the last eight quarters: the beat or miss magnitude, and the next-day move. You are looking for the pattern, not the average.

Some stocks need a large beat simply to hold flat. If a name has beaten by three to five percent four quarters running and fallen twice, the bar is structurally above the printed number and you should assume it still is.

This is the single most useful table you can build before an earnings season, and almost nobody builds it.

3. How it traded into the print

A stock that has run twenty percent in the three weeks before results has pre-paid for good news. The same result lands very differently on a name that sold off into the event.

This is why identical numbers produce opposite reactions in two companies in the same industry on the same day, and it is not a mystery when you check the run-in.

4. What the market is currently rewarding

This one changes, and noticing the change early is where the genuine edge is.

For roughly three years, capital markets rewarded whichever technology company spent the most on AI infrastructure. Capex guides were received as evidence of ambition. In the last six weeks of our coverage period that reversed: the same disclosure — a raised capex guide alongside a beat — has been met with selling.

Nothing about the accounting changed. The question the market was asking changed, from are you committed? to can you fund it, and when does it earn? An analyst still scoring companies on the old question would have read every print this quarter backwards.

Worked example: what we wrote down before AMD reported

We rate AMD Underweight with a $330 target. It reports on 4 August 2026, and consensus sits at roughly $1.61 EPS on about $11.3bn of revenue, up around 47% year on year.

Here is the honest version of the setup, written before the number is known:
Forward P/E~66x
Operating margin~14.4%
The comparison that mattersNVIDIA at ~23x forward with ~66% operating margins

At 66x forward earnings, a revenue beat with flat gross margin is probably not enough. The multiple is not paying for growth — growth is conceded. It is paying for margin expansion that converts that growth into earnings. So the line to watch is not the headline revenue number that will lead the coverage; it is gross margin and the implied incremental margin in the guide.

We have also written down, in public, that this is a genuine risk to our own call in both directions. A clean margin inflection would be evidence against our thesis, and we would rather say that in advance than explain afterwards why it did not count.

That is the discipline the exercise is really for. Deciding what would change your mind before the information arrives is most of what separates a process from an opinion — because afterwards, every outcome feels like it confirms what you already thought.

The question to ask about every position

Before any earnings event, answer this in one sentence:

What, specifically, does this stock need to do to go up?

Not "report good numbers." Something disprovable. Gross margin above 54% with a Q4 guide at or above $12.4bn. If you cannot write the sentence, you do not know what you own into the print — and you will interpret whatever happens as consistent with your thesis, because ambiguity always is.

Three failure modes this catches:

  1. Owning a good company into a bad setup. The business is fine and the bar is unreachable. These are different facts and they are constantly conflated.
  2. Mistaking a re-rating for an operational result. The stock rose because positioning was light, not because the quarter was strong. Learning the wrong lesson here is expensive later.
  3. Scoring yourself on the outcome instead of the reasoning. If you were right for a reason you did not identify in advance, you did not get it right. You got it lucky, and luck does not repeat.

What this does not give you

Being honest about the limits: none of this predicts direction. Knowing the bar is high tells you the risk is asymmetric, not which way the stock will go. Companies clear high bars all the time.

What it does is stop you from being surprised by the mechanism — from concluding that the market is irrational when it has simply been discounting something you had not measured. Over enough prints, the analyst who knows where the bar was makes better decisions than the analyst who only knows where the number landed, even when they forecast the same number.
